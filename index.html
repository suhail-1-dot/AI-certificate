Introduction to Quantity Surveying

Quantity surveying is a professional discipline within the construction industry concerned with the financial and commercial management of construction projects.

A quantity surveyor may become involved from the early stages of a project, when a client is considering whether a proposed development is financially realistic, through design development, procurement, construction and completion.

The role involves much more than simply measuring quantities. Quantity surveyors work with architects, engineers, contractors, clients and other professionals to understand the scope of a project and manage its financial aspects.

Learning objective By the end of this module, learners should understand the basic role of a quantity surveyor and how cost information is used during the different stages of a construction project.

1. What Does a Quantity Surveyor Do?

The responsibilities of a quantity surveyor vary depending on the project, employer and stage of construction.

Typical activities can include:

  • Preparing preliminary cost estimates.
  • Developing cost plans.
  • Measuring construction work.
  • Preparing or reviewing Bills of Quantities.
  • Preparing tender documentation.
  • Analysing contractor tenders.
  • Monitoring project expenditure.
  • Assessing variations to the works.
  • Valuing completed construction work.
  • Assisting with contractual and commercial matters.
  • Preparing or agreeing final accounts.

2. Construction Cost Planning

Cost planning is an important part of the early stages of a construction project. Before construction begins, the client needs to understand whether the proposed project can be delivered within the available budget.

At the beginning of a project, information may be limited. As the design develops, the estimate can become increasingly detailed.

A quantity surveyor may therefore review the cost of different building elements and compare the developing design against the available budget.

For example, changing the specification of finishes, structural systems, mechanical systems or building materials can affect the overall project cost.

3. Measurement in Quantity Surveying

Measurement is one of the fundamental technical activities within quantity surveying.

Construction drawings contain information about the work that needs to be constructed. A quantity surveyor interprets this information and determines quantities that can subsequently be priced.

Depending on the work being measured, quantities may be expressed in different units.

  • Length — metres (m)
  • Area — square metres (m²)
  • Volume — cubic metres (m³)
  • Number — items (nr)
  • Weight — kilograms or tonnes

Standard measurement rules are important because they provide a consistent approach to describing and measuring construction work.

4. Bills of Quantities

A Bill of Quantities, commonly abbreviated as BOQ, is a structured document containing descriptions and measured quantities of work associated with a construction project.

A BOQ can provide contractors with a consistent basis for pricing construction work. It can also provide useful information for subsequent cost management and valuation.

Simple BOQ Example

Item Description Unit Quantity Rate Amount
1 Excavation m³ 25 2,500 62,500
2 Concrete m³ 15 28,000 420,000
3 Blockwork m² 120 4,500 540,000
4 Floor finishes m² 100 6,000 600,000

The example above demonstrates the basic relationship between quantity, unit rate and total amount. In a real project, a BOQ can contain many sections and considerably more detailed descriptions.

5. Estimating Construction Costs

Construction estimating involves predicting the likely cost of carrying out a defined scope of work.

A construction estimate can include many different components, including materials, labour, plant, subcontractor work, preliminaries, overheads, profit and allowances for risks or uncertainties.

The accuracy of an estimate depends heavily on the quality of the available information.

Important concept An early-stage estimate should not automatically be treated as the same thing as a final construction price. Estimates develop as the design and project information become more detailed.

6. Procurement Methods

Procurement describes the approach used to organise the design, construction and contractual relationships within a project.

Different procurement strategies allocate responsibilities and risks differently.

Examples include:

  • Traditional procurement.
  • Design and build.
  • Management contracting.
  • Construction management.

The choice of procurement route can influence cost certainty, programme, risk allocation, design responsibility and the relationship between the client and contractor.

7. Tendering

Tendering is the process through which contractors submit proposals and prices for carrying out defined construction work.

Tender documents normally need to provide sufficient information for contractors to understand what is required.

A quantity surveyor may assist with preparing tender documentation, issuing pricing information, answering commercial queries, analysing submitted prices and preparing recommendations.

8. Cost Control During Construction

Preparing a budget before construction starts is only one part of commercial management.

During construction, the actual financial position of the project needs to be monitored.

Costs can change because of design changes, variations, delays, material price changes, additional work, errors or unforeseen site conditions.

Effective cost control attempts to identify changes early so that the project team can understand their financial consequences.

9. Variations and Changes

Construction projects frequently experience changes after the original scope has been established.

For example, a client may request a different floor finish, an architect may revise a design detail, or a site condition may require additional work.

Such changes can have financial and programme consequences.

The commercial team therefore needs to record, assess and value changes in accordance with the relevant contract procedures.

10. Contract Administration

Construction contracts establish the rights, responsibilities and procedures applicable to the parties involved in a project.

Depending on the contract and role, quantity surveyors may become involved in valuations, payment processes, variations, claims, commercial reporting and final accounts.

Understanding the contract is therefore an important part of professional quantity surveying practice.

11. Lifecycle Costing

The initial construction price is not necessarily the only cost that matters to a building owner.

A building may generate costs throughout its life, including operation, maintenance, repair, replacement and eventual disposal.

Lifecycle costing considers costs over a longer period rather than looking only at the initial construction expenditure.

12. Quantity Surveying and Digital Technology

Digital technology has changed many aspects of construction measurement and cost management.

Building Information Modelling, commonly known as BIM, can provide structured digital information about building elements and systems.

Digital measurement and estimating tools can also help professionals manage large quantities of project information more efficiently.

However, technology does not remove the need for professional judgement. The information being used still needs to be checked, interpreted and understood.

13. Skills Required by a Quantity Surveyor

Quantity surveying combines technical, commercial and communication skills.

Important skills include:

  • Construction knowledge.
  • Measurement.
  • Cost estimating.
  • Numerical accuracy.
  • Contract awareness.
  • Analytical thinking.
  • Report writing.
  • Negotiation.
  • Communication.
  • Attention to detail.
  • Computer and digital skills.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is quantity surveying only about mathematics? Quantity surveying involves numerical work, but it also requires construction knowledge, commercial understanding, communication and analysis.
Do I need construction experience to start learning? No. This introductory course is designed to introduce the basic concepts to beginners.
What is a BOQ? A Bill of Quantities is a structured document containing descriptions and measured quantities of construction work that can be priced.
Where can quantity surveyors work? Quantity surveyors can work for consulting practices, contractors, developers, government organisations and other organisations involved in construction and property development.
Is this course suitable for students? Yes. The material is intended as an introductory learning resource for students and beginners interested in construction cost management.
